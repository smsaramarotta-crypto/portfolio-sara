01 · BRAND & AUDIENCE

Defining who is speaking before deciding what to say

Voci is a media brand created to rebalance a media narrative that leaves little space for women and their contribution to society. Its vision and mission converge on one goal: support a fairer, more inclusive narrative and give visibility to people contributing to change.

The editorial project therefore starts with the audience. The target audience is mainly women and non-binary people aged 16 to 40, with interests in politics, activism, social justice, the environment, feminism, art and independent information.

Recurring needs include reliability, accessibility, representation and plural perspectives: information should be complete, inclusive and able to reduce the need to look elsewhere for missing context or viewpoints.

TARGET

Women and non-binary people aged 16 to 40

Profile

High-school and university students and people who mainly work in social fields.

Interests

Social justice, politics, feminism, the environment, art and independent content.

Reliable, inclusive and accessible information, free from simplifications and dominant filters.

02 · CONTENT RESEARCH

Choosing the theme from audience interests

The theme emerged from the intersection of Voci’s positioning, audience data and research into online questions. The demographic data used in the project shows a large audience sensitive to gender inequalities, with high levels of education but still exposed to economic and representational disparities.

Google Trends was used to observe interest in the role of women in STEM. Also Asked, Answer the Public and analysis of the #HerStory hashtag helped identify recurring questions, overlooked figures and under-recognised scientific contributions.

This led to the long-form theme: three science-related stories, including non-Western contexts, to question how knowledge is narrated and attributed.

01 Audience signal

Interest in equality, representation and critical readings of dominant narratives.

02 Research trend

Growing attention to the role of women in STEM and to figures left out of the best-known narratives.

03 Editorial direction

Tell stories of overlooked inventions and knowledge, including women’s and non-Western perspectives.

03 · VOICE & COPY STRATEGY

Building a recognisable voice before writing

Voci’s value proposition centres context, questions and plural perspectives. It does not propose a single truth: it shifts the centre of the conversation, makes complex topics accessible and treats information as a form of participation and change.

The tone of voice is defined through four qualities: originality, interest, neutrality and depth. The register remains direct and accessible while retaining a critical and recognisable editorial position.

Language choice is also part of the system: the presentation and blog post use ə in the singular and 3 in the plural to include non-binary gender identities.

01 Originality

New and alternative perspectives compared with mainstream narratives.

02 Interest

Content for people who want to explore different ideas and broaden their perspective.

03 Neutrality

Balanced information, built by avoiding prejudice and simplification.

04 Depth

A critical reading that invites a more nuanced understanding of reality.

FINAL ARTICLE Oops, it was a woman’s work: 3 scientific discoveries made by women (but never recognised) by Sara Marotta · 26 June 2025
Read the article Close the article +

Witches, assistants or weavers? When women’s voices in science were silenced… but their results were not.

They healed, but it was magic. They did science, but remained assistants. They encoded, but it was art.

Being a woman was synonymous with exclusion from science.
Being non-white meant total invisibility.

Nobel Prizes, research, discoveries and innovations… everything seems to end up in the hands of men or those who hold power.

And women? They were relegated to secondary roles: homemakers, assistants, craftspeople. When they came from marginalised cultures, the treatment was even more violent: erased, suppressed, their knowledge stolen and hidden behind the veil of “official” science.

As if their work were merely the fertile ground from which men, protected by privilege and power, could “make history”.

But what if the minds behind some scientific revolutions were actually them — those forgotten women?

This phenomenon has a name: the Matilda Effect. It is what happens when women’s scientific achievements are systematically attributed to men, erasing their role from history books.

Behind all this is a history of power that excluded many women from science, hiding their contributions beneath gender and cultural prejudice. It also involves epistemicide — the colonisation of knowledge — the idea that only science produced in Europe or North America is valid or “true”, while knowledge developed elsewhere or in different cultures is ignored, diminished or erased. Science has never been only research: it is also a carefully controlled narrative that decides who has the right to be heard — and who can be forgotten.

And these dynamics are not only a memory of the past: they still exist today, in subtler forms that are no less real.

When I was younger, I was convinced that I was not “suited” to science. I thought I did not have an analytical mind and was not intelligent enough for certain subjects. But this idea did not come from nowhere: it is the result of a narrative that continues to make women feel out of place in STEM, excluding them or pushing them to the margins from the very beginning.

Unsurprisingly, a European Commission study shows that girls have less confidence in themselves than boys in science and mathematics.

Why? Because they feel out of place. Because role models are missing. Because even today, women are an exception in STEM.

That is precisely why I want to tell three stories today. Three brilliant scientific discoveries made by women whom history has “forgotten”.

Voci was born with a clear promise: to tell history as it really happened. Without filters, without Western distortions, and above all without leaving anyone behind. An intersectional narrative that gives space to people too often erased, ignored or relegated to the footnotes. An approach that has resonated with many people looking for a more authentic, critical and inclusive view of reality, creating an attentive, engaged community eager to change how stories are told.

I have been reflecting on these dynamics for a long time: during my studies and through many personal journeys, I have tried to question the versions of reality we take for granted. I have learned to recognise how some forms of knowledge are valued more than others simply because they come from a particular part of the world — and how important it is to listen to the voices that have always remained at the margins.

Spinners or pioneers of binary programming?

Before computers, before Leibniz, before written mathematics — there were people already encoding the world. But they did not do it with pen and calculation. They did it with knots. And with their hands.

The khipu, used by Inca peoples and preserved for centuries in the Andes, were far more than an ethnographic curiosity. They were memory devices and recording systems based on binary principles: a twist to the right or left, a simple or complex knot, presence or absence. Recent research, including work published by Sabine Hyland and Gary Urton, suggests that they operated through a logic comparable to the foundations of digital binary code. Yes: the language that powers every computer today.

For centuries they were called “craft” or “folklore”. Never science. Never language. But the facts tell another story. Khipu were a real coding system:

  • each knot had a precise value,
  • each twist had a direction,
  • each colour had a function,
  • each binary choice (Z or S, above or below, left or right) followed positional logic.

Research by var3 studios3 has shown that khipu contained complex binary structures, very similar to modern programming languages. In other words: it was programming. Without keyboards. Without servers. Without Europe.

Just as in modern binary code, each choice here was a unit of information. A bit.

And no, men were not the only people to use these systems. Inca women were central: they wove, read and passed on the khipu. Some were made by women for women’s ceremonies, while others were connected to religious or social functions directly related to their experience.

They did not assist. They did not decorate. They encoded.

But when those objects entered the hands of European “experts”, everything changed. They were no longer tools of knowledge. They became “ethnic curiosities”, “ritual decorations” and “remains of lost knowledge”.

Why? Because science — the science recognised, legitimised and narrated — was built with very precise boundaries:

  • it must speak the right language (preferably Latin or academic English)
  • it must come from the right places (Europe, the United States)
  • it must have the right face (male, white, with a doctorate)

So today, the name Leibniz remains in history books, while the collective intelligence that created the khipu disappears into the footnotes — or not even there.

But you do not need a laboratory to work with information.
You do not need a keyboard to write code.
Binary logic already existed. And it speaks to us in a feminine voice, in Quechua, and through hands that weave threads.

And telling this story is not only an act of historical justice.
It is a reminder that technology was never born in one place or from one gender alone.
It is saying that this was science too. And that this too — was a woman’s work.

Generative minds or just assistants?

Tsuneko Okazaki is one of those names rarely found in schoolbooks — even though her research entered DNA. Literally. Together with her husband Reiji, she discovered that DNA does not replicate in a linear way, but in fragments.

Those “Okazaki fragments” are now cited in every molecular biology textbook. But when that discovery is discussed, only one name remains. Reiji.

Yet it was Tsuneko herself who continued the work after her husband’s death. Not to honour an inheritance, but because the research was hers too. It was part of her vision, method and scientific intelligence. The sources make this clear: she continued the experiments, led the laboratory, signed key publications and consolidated the findings that now give her name to an entire cellular process (source).

Yet here too, the narrative did its work: it stitched her into the silent role of the “wife of”. A presence that accompanies but does not generate. That observes but does not lead. That follows but does not think. It is a familiar script, repeated with remarkable consistency:

  • if a woman works beside a man, she is the assistant;
  • if she contributes to a discovery, she “helped”;
  • if she continues alone, she “carries on someone else’s work”.

And so Tsuneko’s name remained at the margins, as this feature also recalls, describing how long she was ignored in awards, citations and even academic memory. Despite the amount of data collected, despite her dedication, without her we could not even explain today how the genome is copied.

Look beyond the labels and it becomes clear that she was not behind the scenes. Tsuneko designed experiments, interpreted results and built new knowledge. She was absolutely a generative mind. Yet in the history of science, space for women continues to be written in the margins. As if authentic knowledge needed only one voice. More authoritative. More recognised. More masculine.

Yet something is shifting today. Tsuneko Okazaki received several awards in later years, was celebrated by the Japanese scientific community and chose to use that visibility not to retell the past, but to make space for others. She supported programmes for young women researchers and promoted women’s access to academic careers, reminding us through determination and example that knowledge has a thousand voices. We only need to listen.

The Okazaki fragments tell her story too.
And this too — was a woman’s work.

Non-scientific knowledge or simply unrecognised knowledge?

Lakota women, the Oceti Sakowin of North America, have known for generations that gut health is connected to neurological imbalances caused by trauma. To address these imbalances, they prescribe eating fibre-rich bison intestines, an ancient remedy deeply rooted in their embodied experience and collective culture.

It is not a laboratory discovery, nor an idea born from patient studies or statistical analysis, yet it speaks to a real and complex relationship between body and mind, trauma and physical health.

This knowledge, however, was confined for a long time to margins considered “unscientific”, simply because:

  • it did not meet the strict criteria of Western science, based on experimental data and academic publications;
  • it was intertwined with Indigenous cultural and spiritual practices, which “official” science has historically struggled to recognise as valid;
  • it was passed down orally, through the body and community, rather than through texts or university conferences.

Modern medicine now recognises the importance of the gut-brain connection and is increasingly interested in the impact of trauma on the body. But it often does so while ignoring the origins of these insights and the fact that they were already part of Lakota women’s knowledge, knowledge that remained invisible for far too long.

This is not only a matter of cultural pride or recognition. It demonstrates that knowledge is never neutral and that official science, with its criteria and privileges, has always chosen which voices to include and which to exclude.

Often, the very people who preserve this alternative knowledge have been excluded, marginalised or reduced to mere folk tradition, without receiving the space and legitimacy they deserve.

Remembering this means broadening the concept of science and embracing a more inclusive narrative that recognises the value of practices and knowledge different from Western ones, and above all recognises the work and voices of the women behind these discoveries.

This too — was a woman’s work.

Conclusion

Women have done science. Women in the Andes encoded binary systems long before computers, Tsuneko Okazaki reshaped our understanding of DNA replication, and Lakota women connected gut health and trauma long before Western medicine addressed it. Yet all of them — and so many others — have been forgotten, ignored or even robbed of credit.

How many other discoveries and brilliant minds have been left out of history books? Science has never been an objective and neutral story, but a narrative built by those who hold power — often male, Western and white.

To truly see, we must learn to deconstruct: dismantle prejudice, open ourselves to more perspectives and move beyond Eurocentric and patriarchal filters.

This is where Voci begins: a project that offers information without bias, without requiring you to search through a thousand sources to understand what is true.

Here you will find broad overviews, different voices and perspectives that challenge dominant narratives, with a decentered approach grounded in cultural relativism — because none of us has a single view, but we can all learn to look from more than one angle.

Thank you for making it this far and for wanting to listen to voices that were silenced for too long. Your support is what makes this journey together possible.
